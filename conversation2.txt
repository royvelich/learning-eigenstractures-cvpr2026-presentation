Clarified DCT misconception and weighed technical depth appropriately

Short answer: no, not the Fourier basis. JPEG uses the Discrete Cosine Transform (specifically
DCT-II), and there are two distinct senses in which your phrasing is off.

It's DCT, not DFT. The DCT uses a purely real cosine basis rather than the complex exponentials
of the Fourier transform. They're closely related -- the DCT-II of a length- signal is equivalent
(up to scaling) to the DFT of the signal symmetrically extended to length 2. That symmetric
(even) extension is the whole point: it makes the implied periodic extension continuous at the
block boundaries, killing the boundary discontinuities that a plain DFT's periodic wraparound
would introduce. Those discontinuities would dump energy into high frequencies (Gibbs-style
ringing in the spectrum), which is exactly what you don't want when you're trying to compact
energy into few coefficients. The DCT also gives you real coefficients, and for a first-order
Markov image model it closely approximates the Karhunen­Loève transform (the optimal
decorrelating basis), which is why it's a good fixed proxy for the data-dependent optimum.

**We don't compute the basis from the image.** This is the more important conceptual
correction. The basis is *fixed and known in advance* -- for 8×8 blocks it's the same 64 cosine
basis functions (outer products of 1D cosines at 8 frequencies) regardless of the image. JPEG
just projects each block onto that pre-determined basis; it's an analysis/inner-product step
against fixed vectors, not an eigendecomposition or any per-image basis estimation. Contrast
this with KLT/PCA, where you'd actually compute the basis (eigenvectors of the covariance)
from data. The DCT trades that optimality for a fixed, separable, (log )-factorizable
transform that needs no per-image computation and no transmission of basis vectors.

So the pipeline is: partition into 8×8 blocks  project each onto the fixed DCT basis  quantize
coefficients (this is where the lossy compression actually happens, weighted by perceptual
sensitivity)  entropy-code. The transform itself is lossless and basis-free in the sense that
nothing is learned or computed from the image.

The "Fourier basis of the image" framing would only really apply to something like computing the
2D DFT of the whole image -- which JPEG deliberately doesn't do, both for the boundary
reasons above and because global frequency content is far less useful than localized block
statistics for natural images.

Yes, but it's worth being precise about which transform and where, because the honest answer
is that the pure complex Fourier basis (DFT) is used less often than you might expect for the final
compression step, while close relatives dominate.
The core tension is the one from your JPEG question: the DFT's implicit periodic extension
creates boundary discontinuities that spread energy across the spectrum, hurting energy
compaction. So for coding (storing/transmitting with few coefficients), people overwhelmingly
reach for the DCT or wavelets instead, which avoid this. But the DFT shows up heavily
in analysis stages of compression pipelines, where you're not directly quantizing the DFT
coefficients for storage.

Where genuine Fourier-family transforms do the compression work:

Audio (this is the big one). MP3, AAC, Vorbis, Opus all sit on the MDCT (Modified DCT) -- a
lapped, critically-sampled cosine transform, so still cosine-basis, not complex exponentials,
but very much Fourier-family. The lapping with overlapping windows is the key trick: it removes
the blocking artifacts that plague the non-overlapped block DCT in JPEG, which matters more
for audio where discontinuities are audible clicks. So your everyday music
compression is essentially Fourier-basis compression.

Where the actual complex DFT/FFT earns its place:

The FFT is genuinely used inside compression, but usually as machinery rather than as the
quantized representation. Examples: psychoacoustic models in audio codecs compute an FFT
to estimate masking thresholds (deciding how coarsely to quantize MDCT coefficients), even
though the coded coefficients are MDCT. Speech codecs (CELP-family, used in cellular and
VoIP) use Fourier/LPC-domain analysis for the spectral envelope. Fast convolution and
polynomial multiplication via FFT show up in codec internals.

There are cases where DFT-domain coefficients are directly the compressed representation --
e.g., some scientific/seismic data compression, certain spectral methods, and FFT-based
compression of smooth simulation fields. But for natural images and video, DCT/wavelet win
decisively.

Wavelets are the other major family and are arguably the "right" answer for images when you
want a single global transform: JPEG 2000 uses the discrete wavelet transform, and wavelets
give you multiresolution + good localization in both space and frequency, sidestepping the
block-boundary problem entirely. They're Fourier-adjacent in spirit (basis projection +
quantization) but not Fourier basis.

The contemporary asterisk -- relevant to your field: learned/neural codecs (the autoencoder-
based image and video compression line, e.g. the Ballé hyperprior work and descendants) have
been steadily eating into the transform-coding paradigm. There the "basis" is learned
analysis/synthesis transforms (nonlinear, data-adaptive), trained end-to-end with a rate-
distortion loss and a learned entropy model. This is closer in philosophy to the KLT/PCA
"compute the basis from data" idea than to any fixed Fourier basis -- and at this point neural
codecs beat or match the best hand-designed codecs (VVC/HEVC intra) on perceptual metrics.
There's also recent interest in Fourier features and frequency-domain inductive biases inside
these networks, but that's the network's internal representation, not the coded bitstream.

So the clean summary: cosine-basis transforms (DCT/MDCT) dominate real lossy
compression, wavelets cover the multiresolution niche, the complex FFT is mostly
analysis machinery rather than the stored representation, and learned nonlinear
transforms are the frontier. The pure Fourier basis as the thing-you-quantize is real but
comparatively niche, for exactly the boundary/energy-compaction reasons that pushed JPEG to
the DCT in the first place.

Why this gap exists comes down to three properties you're implicitly optimizing for when you
pick a compression basis: energy compaction (few large coefficients), good behavior at signal
boundaries, and decorrelation close to the KLT optimum for natural-signal statistics. The DCT
wins the first two against the DFT for the typical first-order-Markov statistics of natural signals,
which is why it, not the DFT, became the workhorse.
